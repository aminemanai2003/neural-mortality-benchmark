\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We benchmark 15 mortality forecasting models---8 classical models implemented from scratch, 6 neural network architectures, and an original hybrid---on 8 countries from the Human Mortality Database (1950--2023). Rolling-origin validation with actuarial metrics (life expectancy $e_0$, annuity factor $\ddot{a}_{65}$) addresses questions that existing studies leave open: which model to use with short historical data, which is robust to mortality shocks such as COVID-19, and when the added complexity of neural networks is justified. We introduce \textbf{LC-ResNet}, a hybrid that couples a Poisson Lee--Carter skeleton with a neural residual correction shrunk by $e^{-\lambda h}$ over the forecast horizon $h$, preserving long-run stability and interpretability while capturing short-term non-linearities. Key findings: the age-specific random walk with drift outperforms every structural model on log-rate accuracy at all horizons; recurrent $\kappa_t$ replacements improve on classical Lee--Carter at short horizons but degrade sharply on actuarial metrics at $h=20$; during the COVID-19 shock, models that do \emph{not} extrapolate a trend are the most robust; and LC-ResNet is the most accurate structural model at $h=1$ and for young ages. On a 1{,}000-life French annuity portfolio, the inter-model provision spread reaches 6.3 million EUR, and the Solvency~II longevity SCR varies by 7\% across models---model choice is a capital decision.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Longevity risk---the risk that policyholders live longer than expected---is a primary concern for life insurers and pension funds. Under Solvency~II, the capital requirement for longevity risk typically represents 5--15\% of the best estimate of annuity provisions, so the choice of mortality model is a capital allocation decision rather than a purely academic one.

The Lee--Carter family \citep{lee1992modeling} has dominated actuarial practice for three decades. Neural network approaches have shown promising results in recent literature \citep{richman2019neural,nigri2019deep,perla2021brief}, yet no study offers a practical guide for choosing between classical and neural models given the constraints practitioners actually face: data availability, forecast horizon, age segment, and regulatory interpretability.

This paper fills that gap with four contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A comprehensive benchmark} of 15 models (8 classical, 6 neural, 1 hybrid) on 8 countries, evaluated with statistical \emph{and} actuarial metrics under rolling-origin validation (Sections~\ref{sec:models}--\ref{sec:results}).
    \item \textbf{LC-ResNet}, an original hybrid coupling a Poisson Lee--Carter skeleton with a neural residual network under horizon-dependent shrinkage (Section~\ref{sec:lcresnet}).
    \item \textbf{A decision framework} mapping history length, horizon, age segment, and interpretability needs to a recommended model (Section~\ref{sec:decision}).
    \item \textbf{An actuarial case study} pricing a French annuity portfolio and quantifying model risk in EUR under a Solvency~II longevity shock (Section~\ref{sec:casestudy}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\citet{lee1992modeling} introduced the seminal bilinear model for U.S.\ mortality. Extensions include the $e_0$-adjusted variant of \citet{lee2001evaluating}, the fitting-period selection of \citet{booth2002applying}, the Poisson likelihood formulation of \citet{brouhns2002poisson}, the two-factor CBD model for older ages \citep{cairns2006two}, and the functional data approach of \citet{hyndman2007robust}. \citet{booth2006demographic} review this literature and document the surprising strength of na\"ive extrapolation.

On the neural side, \citet{nigri2019deep} replace the random-walk forecast of $\kappa_t$ with an LSTM; \citet{richman2019neural} propose a feed-forward network with entity embeddings for multi-population mortality; \citet{perla2021brief} treat mortality forecasting as time-series regression with convolutional and recurrent architectures.

Our work differs in three ways: (i)~all approaches are benchmarked under identical conditions---same data, same rolling origins, same hyperparameter budget; (ii)~evaluation includes actuarial metrics and an EUR-denominated case study rather than log-rate accuracy alone; (iii)~we propose a hybrid model and a practical decision framework.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

We use $1 \times 1$ central death rates $m_{x,t}$ and exposures $E_{x,t}$ from the Human Mortality Database (HMD)\footnote{\url{https://www.mortality.org}. HMD data are free but not redistributable; the repository documents how to download them.} for eight countries: France, England \& Wales, USA, Japan, Italy, Spain, Sweden, and the Netherlands. Ages span 0--100, years 1950--2023 (total population). The window includes the COVID-19 shock (2020--2023), used as a natural out-of-sample stress test.

The central death rate is defined as
\begin{equation}
    m_{x,t} = \frac{D_{x,t}}{E_{x,t}},
\end{equation}
where $D_{x,t}$ denotes deaths at age $x$ in year $t$ and $E_{x,t}$ the matching central exposure. Zero or missing rates are floored at $10^{-6}$ before taking logarithms. Countries whose series end before 2023 (e.g.\ England \& Wales, last available year 2022) have trailing missing years trimmed rather than imputed.

\section{Models}
\label{sec:models}

All classical models are implemented from scratch in Python (NumPy/SciPy); neural models use PyTorch. Every model implements a common interface (\texttt{fit}, \texttt{forecast}, \texttt{simulate}), so the evaluation harness treats all 15 identically.

\subsection{Classical models}

\textbf{Lee--Carter (LC).} The bilinear structure
\begin{equation}
    \ln m_{x,t} = a_x + b_x\,\kappa_t + \varepsilon_{x,t},
\end{equation}
estimated by SVD under the constraints $\sum_x b_x = 1$ and $\sum_t \kappa_t = 0$, with $\kappa_t$ re-estimated on observed death counts. The index $\kappa_t$ is forecast by a random walk with drift.

\textbf{Lee--Miller (LM).} Adjusts $\kappa_t$ each year so that the model-implied life expectancy matches the observed $e_0$ \citep{lee2001evaluating}.

\textbf{Booth--Maindonald--Smith (BMS).} Selects the fitting period by testing the linearity of $\kappa_t$, then applies LC on the selected window \citep{booth2002applying}.

\textbf{Poisson Lee--Carter (PLC).} Assumes $D_{x,t} \sim \text{Poisson}\!\left(E_{x,t}\, e^{a_x + b_x \kappa_t}\right)$ and maximizes the likelihood by alternating Newton--Raphson updates \citep{brouhns2002poisson}.

\textbf{Cairns--Blake--Dowd (CBD).} For ages 60--100,
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{logit} q_{x,t} = \kappa_t^{(1)} + \kappa_t^{(2)}\,(x - \bar{x}),
\end{equation}
with a bivariate random walk with drift on $(\kappa_t^{(1)}, \kappa_t^{(2)})$ \citep{cairns2006two}.

\textbf{Hyndman--Ullah (HU).} Penalized-spline smoothing of the age profiles, functional principal component decomposition ($K=6$), and ARIMA forecasting of the scores \citep{hyndman2007robust}.

\textbf{Baselines.} An age-specific random walk with drift on $\ln m_{x,t}$, and frozen rates (last observed year held constant). Honest benchmarks require both.

\subsection{Neural models}

For benchmark fairness, every neural model uses the same budget: 200 epochs, early stopping with patience 20, Adam with learning rate $10^{-3}$ and weight decay $10^{-4}$, fixed seed. Architectures are detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:configs}.

\textbf{Neural $\kappa_t$ models.} The LC skeleton $(a_x, b_x)$ is retained; only the $\kappa_t$ forecast is replaced by an LSTM, GRU, Bi-LSTM, or a two-layer Transformer encoder operating on a 20-year lookback window.

\textbf{FFNN with embeddings.} Following \citet{richman2019neural}: an age embedding concatenated with a continuous normalized year feature, mapped by a three-layer perceptron directly to $\ln m_{x,t}$. The continuous year encoding---rather than a year embedding---is what allows extrapolation to unseen future years.

\textbf{CNN on the mortality surface.} The $(\text{age} \times \text{year})$ matrix is treated as an image; three convolutional layers predict the next annual profile from a sliding window, applied recursively for multi-step forecasts.

\subsection{LC-ResNet: a hybrid with horizon shrinkage}
\label{sec:lcresnet}

Our proposed hybrid preserves the interpretable LC skeleton and lets a small network correct only what the skeleton misses:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Fit a Poisson Lee--Carter, obtaining $\hat{a}_x, \hat{b}_x, \hat{\kappa}_t$.
    \item Compute the structured residuals $r_{x,t} = \ln m_{x,t} - (\hat{a}_x + \hat{b}_x \hat{\kappa}_t)$.
    \item Train a residual network $f_\theta$ (MLP 64--32) on normalized features $(\tilde{x}, \tilde{t}, \tilde{b}_x, \tilde{\kappa}_t)$ to predict $r_{x,t}$.
    \item Forecast with horizon-dependent shrinkage:
\end{enumerate}
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\ln m}_{x,T+h}
    \;=\;
    \underbrace{\hat{a}_x + \hat{b}_x\,\hat{\kappa}_{T+h}}_{\text{LC skeleton}}
    \;+\;
    e^{-\lambda h}\,
    \underbrace{f_\theta\!\left(\tilde{x},\, \widetilde{T{+}h},\, \tilde{b}_x,\, \tilde{\kappa}_{T+h}\right)}_{\text{neural correction}},
    \qquad \lambda = 0.1 .
    \label{eq:lcresnet}
\end{equation}

At $h = 1$ the correction is almost fully active ($e^{-0.1} \approx 0.90$); at $h = 20$ it has decayed to $e^{-2} \approx 0.14$ and the model reverts to the stable LC extrapolation. This directly targets the main failure mode of neural mortality models---long-horizon extrapolation instability---while $(a_x, b_x, \kappa_t)$ remain available for regulatory reporting.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\textbf{Rolling-origin validation.} For each origin $T_0 \in \{1990, 1992, \ldots, 2018\}$, every model is re-trained from scratch on years $[1950, T_0]$ and evaluated on the following $h \in \{1, 5, 10, 20\}$ years, whenever the truth is available.

\textbf{Metrics.} RMSE on $\ln m_{x,t}$; RMSE on period life expectancy at birth $e_0$ (in years); RMSE on the whole-life annuity-due factor $\ddot{a}_{65}$ at interest rate $i = 2\%$. Actuarial metrics require the full age grid, so CBD (ages 60+) is evaluated on log-rates only.

\textbf{Significance.} Pairwise Diebold--Mariano tests \citep{diebold1995comparing} with the Harvey--Leybourne--Newbold small-sample and multi-step correction \citep{harvey1997testing}, referenced against a Student-$t$ distribution.

\textbf{Scenarios.} Three targeted experiments feed the decision framework: (i)~\emph{short history}---training truncated to the last 20, 30, or 50 years before the origin; (ii)~\emph{mortality shock}---training up to 2019, evaluation on 2020--2022 (COVID-19); (iii)~\emph{age groups}---separate evaluation on ages 0--19, 20--64, and 65--100.

\noindent\textbf{Key findings.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The \textbf{age-specific random walk with drift dominates every structural model} on log-rate accuracy at every horizon (0.100 at $h{=}1$ to 0.203 at $h{=}20$)---a sobering baseline result consistent with \citet{booth2006demographic}.
    \item Among structural models, \textbf{LC-ResNet is the most accurate at $h{=}1$} (0.120) and stays competitive throughout, matching the LSTM/GRU at $h{=}5$ and beating every neural model at $h{=}20$ (0.247), where the shrinkage has handed control back to the LC skeleton.

    \item \textbf{Frozen rates collapse with horizon} (0.100 $\to$ 0.314), as expected for a no-trend baseline: mortality does improve over 20 years.
\end{enumerate}

The central observation is an \textbf{inversion between statistical and actuarial accuracy for recurrent models}: the LSTM beats Lee--Carter on log-rates at $h \le 10$ (Table~\ref{tab:rmse_logmx}) yet its $e_0$ error at $h{=}20$ is 2.06 years against 0.74 for Lee--Miller. \subsection{Short history and age segments}

With training truncated to 20 years, LC-ResNet is the best full-grid structural model (RMSE 0.159 versus 0.165 for LC and 0.173 for the random walk); frozen rates again lead among baselines (0.134). For \textbf{young ages (0--19)}, LC-ResNet ranks first overall (0.228), suggesting the residual network captures infant- and child-mortality non-linearities that the bilinear form misses. For \textbf{elderly ages (65--100)}---the annuity segment---simple approaches win: frozen rates (0.054), Hyndman--Ullah and the random walk (0.068). Full tables appear in Appendix~\ref{app:extra}.

\section{Decision Framework}
\label{sec:decision}

\end{table}

The longevity SCR ranges from 11.52 to 12.33 M\,EUR---a 7\% relative spread---while the SCR-to-provision ratio is stable (5.2--5.8\%). Model choice therefore shifts both the best estimate and the regulatory capital, but the shock multiplier itself is robust to specification.

% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{The random walk result.} The strongest single finding is that an age-specific random walk with drift beats every structural model on log-rate accuracy at every horizon. Structure helps not by improving raw accuracy but by improving \emph{actuarial} accuracy ($e_0$, $\ddot{a}_{65}$), where cross-age consistency matters---see the contrast between Tables~\ref{tab:rmse_logmx} and~\ref{tab:actuarial}.

\textbf{When neural networks help, and how they fail.} Recurrent $\kappa_t$ models genuinely improve short-horizon log-rate accuracy, and they adapt quickly during the COVID shock. Their failure mode is systematic long-horizon bias that compounds through the life table: a 2-year $e_0$ error at $h{=}20$ is disqualifying for provisioning. Model complexity must be matched to the decision the forecast serves.

\textbf{Bounded flexibility works.} LC-ResNet's design principle---let a neural network correct the skeleton where evidence is dense (short horizons, young ages) and fade it out where extrapolation risk dominates---delivers the intended profile: best structural model at $h{=}1$ and for young ages, classical-level actuarial accuracy at $h{=}20$, and full interpretability of the skeleton.

\textbf{Limitations.} All models are single-population; cohort effects are not explicitly modeled; neural results use a single seed rather than deep ensembles; prediction-interval calibration is not evaluated here; and post-2023 data will be needed to judge post-COVID trend re-establishment.

% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

When should actuaries trust neural networks for mortality forecasting? On this evidence: at short horizons, with long training histories, for log-rate objectives---and even then, preferably inside a constrained design such as LC-ResNet, where the neural component is bounded and fades with horizon. For long-horizon provisioning, the classical family (Lee--Miller in particular) remains the safer instrument, and the random walk with drift should always be run as the honesty check. The 6.3 M\,EUR provision spread on a modest portfolio shows that these choices are, ultimately, capital decisions.
