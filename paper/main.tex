\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We benchmark 14 mortality forecasting implementations---5 classical structural models, 2 simple baselines, 6 neural architectures, and a proposed hybrid---on 8 countries from the Human Mortality Database (1950--2023). Rolling-origin validation uses log-rate and age-100-truncated actuarial metrics to study short histories, forecast horizons, age segments, and the COVID-19 shock. \textbf{LC-ResNet} couples a Poisson Lee--Carter skeleton with a neural residual correction shrunk by $e^{-\lambda h}$ at horizon $h$. Key findings are benchmark-specific: the age-specific random walk with drift has the lowest full-grid log-rate error at every horizon; recurrent $\kappa_t$ replacements improve on Lee--Carter at short horizons but deteriorate sharply on 20-year actuarial metrics; frozen rates are most robust over the cumulative 2020--2022 shock window; and LC-ResNet has the lowest one-year error among structured models. On a 1{,}000-life French annuity portfolio valued from 2033 rates, the five-model provision spread is 6.26 million EUR and the liability increase under a 20\% mortality-rate stress varies by 7\% across models. The results caution against treating model complexity as a substitute for objective-specific validation.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Longevity risk---the risk that policyholders live longer than expected---is a primary concern for life insurers and pension funds. Under the Solvency~II standard formula, the longevity-risk stress is an instantaneous permanent 20\% decrease in mortality rates \citep{eiopa2026article138}. The choice of mortality model is therefore relevant to both best-estimate liabilities and capital.

The Lee--Carter family \citep{lee1992modeling} has dominated mortality forecasting for three decades. Recent work has expanded the toolkit through neural networks, shrinkage, multi-task learning, Bayesian model averaging, and variational inference \citep{richman2019neural,barigou2023bma,li2025boosting,demori2025multitask,andersson2026variational}. The remaining practical issue is not whether flexible models can forecast mortality, but when their added complexity improves the particular quantity an actuary needs.

This paper fills that gap with four contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A reproducible benchmark} of 14 implementations on 8 countries, evaluated with log-rate and actuarial metrics under rolling-origin validation (Sections~\ref{sec:models}--\ref{sec:results}).
    \item \textbf{LC-ResNet}, a proposed hybrid coupling a Poisson Lee--Carter skeleton with a neural residual network under horizon-dependent shrinkage (Section~\ref{sec:lcresnet}).
    \item \textbf{Objective-specific guidance} mapping history length, horizon, age segment, and transparency constraints to evidence from this benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:decision}).
    \item \textbf{An actuarial case study} pricing a French annuity portfolio and quantifying model risk in EUR under the 20\% mortality-rate reduction used in the Solvency~II longevity stress (Section~\ref{sec:casestudy}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\citet{lee1992modeling} introduced the seminal bilinear model for U.S.\ mortality. Extensions include the $e_0$-adjusted variant of \citet{lee2001evaluating}, fitting-period and jump-off refinements \citep{booth2002applying}, the Poisson likelihood formulation of \citet{brouhns2002poisson}, the two-factor CBD model for older ages \citep{cairns2006two}, and the functional data approach of \citet{hyndman2007robust}. \citet{booth2006demographic} reviews this literature and documents the surprising strength of na\"ive extrapolation.

On the neural side, \citet{nigri2019deep} replace the random-walk forecast of $\kappa_t$ with an LSTM; \citet{richman2019neural} propose a feed-forward network with entity embeddings; and \citet{perla2021brief} study convolutional and recurrent architectures. More recent studies use shrinkage to combine domain models with machine learning \citep{li2025boosting}, share information through multi-task networks \citep{demori2025multitask}, and increase age-time resolution \citep{zheng2026finegrained}. Forecast combination and uncertainty are developed through leave-future-out Bayesian model averaging \citep{barigou2023bma} and variational inference \citep{andersson2026variational}.

Our scope is deliberately narrower than several of these studies: single-population point forecasts on one harmonized data design. Within that scope, all implementations use the same rolling origins, neural training budget, and evaluation code; the comparison includes actuarial metrics and a EUR-denominated case study; and the proposed hybrid tests whether horizon shrinkage can bound a neural residual correction.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

We use $1 \times 1$ central death rates $m_{x,t}$ and exposures $E_{x,t}$ from the Human Mortality Database (HMD)\footnote{HMD-produced estimates are distributed under CC BY 4.0; source input data can retain provider-specific terms. HMD recommends downloading a current copy and citing the source rather than circulating local copies \citep{hmd2026agreement}.} for eight countries: France, England \& Wales, USA, Japan, Italy, Spain, Sweden, and the Netherlands. Ages span 0--100, years 1950--2023 (total population). The window includes the COVID-19 shock (2020--2023), used as a natural out-of-sample stress test.

The central death rate is defined as
\begin{equation}
    m_{x,t} = \frac{D_{x,t}}{E_{x,t}},
\end{equation}
where $D_{x,t}$ denotes deaths at age $x$ in year $t$ and $E_{x,t}$ the matching central exposure. Zero or missing rates are floored at $10^{-6}$ before taking logarithms. Countries whose series end before 2023 (e.g.\ England \& Wales, last available year 2022) have trailing missing years trimmed rather than imputed.

\section{Models}
\label{sec:models}

The statistical models are implemented in Python (NumPy/SciPy), and neural models use PyTorch. Every implementation exposes a common \texttt{fit}/\texttt{forecast} interface, so the evaluation harness treats all 14 consistently, except that CBD is restricted to ages 60--100.

\subsection{Classical models}

\textbf{Lee--Carter (LC).} The bilinear structure
\begin{equation}
    \ln m_{x,t} = a_x + b_x\,\kappa_t + \varepsilon_{x,t},
\end{equation}
estimated by SVD under the constraints $\sum_x b_x = 1$ and $\sum_t \kappa_t = 0$, with $\kappa_t$ re-estimated on observed death counts. The index $\kappa_t$ is forecast by a random walk with drift.

\textbf{Lee--Miller (LM).} Adjusts $\kappa_t$ each year so that the model-implied life expectancy matches the observed $e_0$ \citep{lee2001evaluating}.

\textbf{Poisson Lee--Carter (PLC).} Assumes $D_{x,t} \sim \text{Poisson}\!\left(E_{x,t}\, e^{a_x + b_x \kappa_t}\right)$ and maximizes the likelihood by alternating Newton--Raphson updates \citep{brouhns2002poisson}.

\textbf{Cairns--Blake--Dowd (CBD).} For ages 60--100,
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{logit} q_{x,t} = \kappa_t^{(1)} + \kappa_t^{(2)}\,(x - \bar{x}),
\end{equation}
with a bivariate random walk with drift on $(\kappa_t^{(1)}, \kappa_t^{(2)})$ \citep{cairns2006two}.

\textbf{Hyndman--Ullah-style functional model (HU).} Penalized-spline smoothing of age profiles, functional principal components ($K=6$), and random-walk-with-drift score forecasts. This is a simplified implementation of the broader robust functional procedure in \citet{hyndman2007robust}.

\textbf{Baselines.} An age-specific random walk with drift on $\ln m_{x,t}$, and frozen rates (last observed year held constant). Honest benchmarks require both.

\subsection{Neural models}

For benchmark fairness, every neural model uses the same budget: 200 epochs, early stopping with patience 20, Adam with learning rate $10^{-3}$ and weight decay $10^{-4}$, fixed seed. Architectures are detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:configs}.

\textbf{Neural $\kappa_t$ models.} The LC skeleton $(a_x, b_x)$ is retained; only the $\kappa_t$ forecast is replaced by an LSTM, GRU, Bi-LSTM, or a two-layer Transformer encoder operating on a 20-year lookback window.

\textbf{FFNN with embeddings.} Following \citet{richman2019neural}: an age embedding concatenated with a continuous normalized year feature, mapped by a three-layer perceptron directly to $\ln m_{x,t}$. The continuous year encoding---rather than a year embedding---is what allows extrapolation to unseen future years.

\textbf{CNN on the mortality surface.} The $(\text{age} \times \text{year})$ matrix is treated as an image; three convolutional layers predict the next annual profile from a sliding window, applied recursively for multi-step forecasts.

\subsection{LC-ResNet: a hybrid with horizon shrinkage}
\label{sec:lcresnet}

Our proposed hybrid retains an interpretable LC skeleton and lets a small network correct what the skeleton misses:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Fit a Poisson Lee--Carter, obtaining $\hat{a}_x, \hat{b}_x, \hat{\kappa}_t$.
    \item Compute the structured residuals $r_{x,t} = \ln m_{x,t} - (\hat{a}_x + \hat{b}_x \hat{\kappa}_t)$.
    \item Train a residual network $f_\theta$ (MLP 64--32) on normalized features $(\tilde{x}, \tilde{t}, \tilde{b}_x, \tilde{\kappa}_t)$ to predict $r_{x,t}$.
    \item Forecast with horizon-dependent shrinkage:
\end{enumerate}
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\ln m}_{x,T+h}
    \;=\;
    \underbrace{\hat{a}_x + \hat{b}_x\,\hat{\kappa}_{T+h}}_{\text{LC skeleton}}
    \;+\;
    e^{-\lambda h}\,
    \underbrace{f_\theta\!\left(\tilde{x},\, \widetilde{T{+}h},\, \tilde{b}_x,\, \tilde{\kappa}_{T+h}\right)}_{\text{neural correction}},
    \qquad \lambda = 0.1 .
    \label{eq:lcresnet}
\end{equation}

At $h = 1$ the correction has weight $e^{-0.1} \approx 0.90$; at $h = 20$ its weight is $e^{-2} \approx 0.14$. The LC parameters remain available for reporting, but the residual correction is a black-box component; the complete hybrid should therefore be described as partially, not fully, interpretable.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\textbf{Rolling-origin validation.} For each origin $T_0 \in \{1990, 1992, \ldots, 2018\}$, every model is re-trained from scratch on years $[1950, T_0]$ and evaluated at $h \in \{1, 5, 10, 20\}$ whenever the truth is available. A reported $h$-step error is calculated over the complete path $T_0{+}1,\ldots,T_0{+}h$, not only the terminal year. Because country end dates differ, each table entry is the unweighted mean of the valid country--origin RMSE values.

\textbf{Metrics.} RMSE on $\ln m_{x,t}$; RMSE on period life expectancy at birth $e_0$ (years); and RMSE on the annuity-due factor $\ddot{a}_{65}$ at $i=2\%$. Both actuarial quantities are valued on the observed 0--100 grid and are therefore truncated at age 100. They are reported only for full-grid forecasts; CBD is evaluated on log-rates over ages 60--100.

\textbf{Scenarios.} Three targeted experiments inform the guidance: (i)~\emph{short history}---one origin per country is chosen so that 10 evaluation years remain, then the training window is restricted to 20, 30, or 50 years and horizons 5 and 10 are averaged; (ii)~\emph{mortality shock}---training ends in 2019 and cumulative paths through 2020, 2021, and 2022 are evaluated; (iii)~\emph{age groups}---each model is refitted separately on ages 0--19, 20--64, and 65--100. We report point-accuracy comparisons only; formal predictive intervals and pairwise significance tests are outside this study.

\noindent\textbf{Key findings.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The \textbf{age-specific random walk with drift dominates every full-grid structural model} on log-rate accuracy at every horizon (0.100 at $h{=}1$ to 0.203 at $h{=}20$)---a sobering baseline result consistent with \citet{booth2006demographic}.     \item Among structured models, \textbf{LC-ResNet is the most accurate at $h{=}1$} (0.120) and ties the LSTM/GRU at $h{=}5$. At $h{=}20$, Lee--Miller is the strongest structured model (0.232), followed by LC-ResNet (0.247).

    \item \textbf{Frozen rates collapse with horizon} (0.100 $\to$ 0.314), as expected for a no-trend baseline: mortality does improve over 20 years.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Short history and age segments}

With 20 training years, frozen rates have the lowest full-grid error (0.134), while LC-ResNet is the best structured model (0.159). On refitted age-band models, LC-ResNet and Bi-LSTM tie for the lowest young-age error after rounding (0.228). For ages 65--100, frozen rates lead (0.054), followed by H-U and the random walk (0.068); CBD records 0.102 in its intended age range. Full tables appear in Appendix~\ref{app:extra}.

The five models are fitted to France through 2023 and the age-specific rates in their 10-year-ahead forecast (2033) are used with $i=2\%$. Payments and survival are valued through age 100. % ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{The random walk result.} The strongest single finding is that an age-specific random walk with drift beats every structured model on log-rate accuracy at every horizon. Yet the winner changes under actuarial losses, showing why a benchmark should include both simple baselines and decision-relevant metrics.

\textbf{When neural networks help, and how they fail.} Recurrent $\kappa_t$ models improve short-horizon log-rate accuracy and are competitive in the COVID experiment, but their long-horizon $e_0$ errors exceed two years. Conversely, the FFNN performs poorly on log-rate RMSE yet leads the 20-year annuity metric. Model complexity must be assessed against the decision-specific loss.

\textbf{Bounded flexibility is promising but not dominant.} LC-ResNet's shrinking correction yields the lowest one-year structured-model error and classical-level 20-year actuarial errors. It ties, rather than uniquely wins, the young-age experiment and does not beat Lee--Miller at 20 years. The skeleton is transparent; the residual correction is not.

\textbf{Limitations.} All models are single-population; cohort effects are not explicit; the H-U implementation uses random-walk score forecasts rather than automatic ARIMA selection; neural results use one fixed seed and one shared training budget rather than model-specific tuning; predictive distributions and statistical significance are not evaluated; and life-table quantities are truncated at age 100. Unequal country end dates create unequal numbers of valid origins, and post-2023 observations are needed to assess post-COVID trends. The case study is stylized and its five-model spread is sensitive to the inclusion of age-restricted CBD. The exact retrieval date of the HMD snapshot behind the stored benchmark was not preserved, so later HMD revisions may produce small differences.

% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

When should actuaries trust neural networks for mortality forecasting? Only after validation against the exact target quantity. Here, neural $\kappa_t$ models help at short horizons but fail on long-horizon actuarial metrics; a feed-forward model nevertheless leads the 20-year annuity metric; and the proposed LC-ResNet offers a useful structured compromise at short horizons. The random walk and frozen-rate baselines remain indispensable. The 6.26 M\,EUR five-model provision spread illustrates why model choice should be reported as a sensitivity, not hidden behind a single ranking.
